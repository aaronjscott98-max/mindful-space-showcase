Understanding Anxiety

Anxiety acts as the body’s over-sensitive alarm system. It is a persistent, physical state that often manifests as a racing heart, shallow breathing, or a constant loop of intrusive thoughts.

Understanding the Physicality

It is important to recognise that anxiety is not merely "worrying" but a complex physiological response. The body releases cortisol and adrenaline, preparing for a threat that often does not exist in the immediate environment. Treating it as a physical experience rather than a character flaw is vital.

The Cycle of Anxiety

Anxiety often operates in a loop: a trigger leads to a physical sensation, which is interpreted as a threat, which leads to further symptoms, reinforcing the cycle. Recognising that this is a loop not an endless state is a vital breakthrough. Identifying early sensations (like tight shoulders or shallow breathing) before the "story" of the anxiety takes over can help interrupt the loop.

A Simple Grounding Technique

When the mind feels trapped, grounding can help bring focus back to the present. The 5-4-3-2-1 method is a gentle way to reconnect with the environment:

  • 5 things you can see around you.
  • 4 things you can touch or feel.
  • 3 things you can hear.
  • 2 things you can smell.
  • 1 thing you can taste (or one positive thought about yourself).
A gentle reminder

You can take things one small step at a time. Seeking help is a valid next step.

Recognising Early Signs

Anxiety does not always look like overt panic. Sometimes, it manifests as irritability, a struggle to focus on simple tasks, or a subtle need to withdraw from social situations. Recognising these smaller, early signs can help in providing support before the "alarm" becomes overwhelming.

Reframing the Narrative

Common Myth: Anxiety is just a sign of weakness or a lack of personal resilience.

The Reality: Anxiety is a complex physiological response. It is not a choice, nor is it a reflection of character. Recognising its legitimacy is the first step toward genuine empathy.

How to Provide Support

  • Active Presence: Often, the most powerful tool is simply being present. Sitting with someone while they experience high anxiety provides a physical anchor.
  • Approaching Conversations: When initiating a conversation, use gentle, open questions. Try: "I’ve noticed things have been tough lately; is there any way I can help make things a little easier for you right now?"
  • Validation over Correction: Avoid phrases that dismiss the experience. Acknowledge the difficulty: "It is clear this is difficult right now, and it is safe to stay here for a while."
  • Co-Regulating Breath: Instead of commanding someone to "breathe," model a slow rhythm yourself inhale for four, hold for four, exhale for six and invite them to join if they feel able.
  • Encouraging Movement: Suggest gentle, rhythmic action like a slow walk or tidying a small space to help the body process excess adrenaline.
  • Normalise the Experience: Remind them that these physical sensations are a natural though uncomfortable biological response, not a sign of personal failure.
  • Post-Anxiety Care: After the peak has passed, offer simple, low pressure comfort like a glass of water or a quiet environment. Avoid asking for analysis of the anxiety until they feel fully settled.
  • Respecting Boundaries: Sometimes, someone needs space to process. Let them know you remain available whenever they are ready to reconnect.

Finding Support and Safety

If someone is at immediate risk of harm, please contact emergency services without delay:

  • Emergency: Call 999 or visit the nearest Accident and Emergency department.
  • Urgent Medical Advice: Call 111 to reach NHS 24.
  • Samaritans: Call 116 123 (Available 24/7).
  • Breathing Space (Scotland): Call 0800 83 85 87 (Mon–Thu 6 PM–2 AM; Fri 6 PM–Mon 6 AM).
